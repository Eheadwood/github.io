How to price a product for profit

Start with your full cost per unit: materials, packaging and shipping, plus your own time. Many small sellers leave their labor out and end up working for free. Then decide the profit margin you want, meaning the share of the selling price you keep as profit.

Platform and card fees are charged on the selling price, not on your cost, so they have to be built in too. The suggested price is your cost divided by (1 − margin − fees). With a cost of 10.70, a 50% margin and 5% fees, that's 10.70 ÷ 0.45 = 23.78.

Margin vs. markup

Margin and markup describe the same profit from two angles. Margin is profit divided by price; markup is profit divided by cost. A 50% margin is a 100% markup, and a 25% margin is only a 33% markup. Mixing them up is one of the most common reasons products end up underpriced.

Break-even: how many do you need to sell?

Your fixed costs, like software, rent and marketing, have to be paid however much you sell. Divide them by the profit you make on each unit to see how many you need to sell each month before you make any money at all. If that number looks unrealistic, raise your price, lower your costs, or both.

Frequently asked questions

What profit margin should I aim for?

It depends on your industry, but many handmade and small product businesses aim for 40–60% after all costs including their own time. Lower margins leave little room for discounts, returns and slow months.

Should I include my time in the cost?

Yes. If you don't pay yourself for your time, your price only covers materials and you're working for free. Use a realistic hourly rate.

Why include platform fees in the price?

Fees like marketplace commissions and card processing are a percentage of the selling price, so they grow when your price grows. Building them into the formula keeps your margin intact.
